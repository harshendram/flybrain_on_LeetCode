      What am I looking at?

      Every dot is one of the 141,781 neurons of a real male fruit fly, at the position of its cell body, from the MaleCNS v1.0 connectome: the complete wiring diagram of a fly's nervous system traced from electron microscopy (Janelia, Google & Cambridge, 2026). Inside glow the real shapes of the 2,208 neurons of its right mushroom body, its centre for learning smells. Each spike travels along the real branches of the neuron that fires it.

      The fly has no idea what code is. We turn a problem's text into a smell: 51 "receptors" (word-topic detectors learned without labels), one per glomerulus of the antennal lobe. From there on everything is the fly's own wiring: projection neurons → 1,880 Kenyon cells. They are wired exactly as measured in the connectome, and the APL neuron lets only the most strongly driven 2.5% fire. Then come the output neurons, whose synapses were trained with dopamine: reward (PAM) or punishment (PPL1), the fly's only learning rule.

      Honest numbers. On 415 newer problems it never saw, its top guess is a correct technique 40% of the time (always guessing "Dynamic Programming" gets 31%). That's the same as a standard classifier given the same 51-channel smell (39%), and below a classifier reading every word (46%). Scrambling its wiring doesn't hurt (40%): for an unnatural task like this, what matters is the architecture, not the fly's specific connections. Which parts of the wiring can matter is the Evolve and Transplant experiment.

      Can it write code? No. This circuit makes one-step associations. It can't produce a sequence. It can only judge.
